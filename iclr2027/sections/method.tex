\section{Primal-Dual Curvature Matching} \label{sec:method}
As discussed in \Cref{sec:bg_allocation}, naively maximizing the total policy improvement in \Cref{eq:bg_telescope} can lead to skewed development
over tasks that should otherwise be treated equally (see \Cref{sec:ablation}).  
We propose the following constraint that prevents any task from falling behind
what would be achieved by a uniform allocation $u = (\frac{1}{K},\ldots,\frac{1}{K})$ by a threshold $\epsilon>0$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:constraint_uniform_baseline}
\sum_{t = 1}^T U_{t, k}\left( u \right) - U_{t, k}(p_t) \leq \epsilon, \quad \forall k\in[K].
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:constraint_uniform_baseline} enforces an upper-limit on the cumulative counterfactual lag. In practice, estimating $U_{t, k}(u)$ requires a parallel run with uniform allocation $u$, which is cost-prohibitive. We relax the parametric assumptions in recent work~\citep{ye2025datamixinglawsoptimizing,chen2025aioli,kang2025autoscalescaleawaredatamixing} to one in which the policy-improvement surface with respect to mixture allocation is approximately concave along linear interpolation paths. We validate this assumption empirically in Appendix~\ref{app:concavity}. 

\begin{assumption}[Directional concavity of improvement response]
\label{asm:concavity}
For every task $k\in[K]$ and iteration $t$, the task-wise utility $U_{t,k}$ admits a
supergradient $g_{t,k}=\nabla U_{t,k}(p_t)$ with respect to the sampling distribution
$p_t$, so that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:constraint_upper_bound}
U_{t, k}\left( u \right) - U_{t, k}(p_t) \leq \langle g_{t, k}, u - p_t \rangle .
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}
\Cref{eq:constraint_uniform_baseline} would be satisfied if we instead impose the RHS of \Cref{eq:constraint_upper_bound}, albeit being more restrictive. This leads to the following constrained optimization problem:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:constrained_optimization_problem}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{p_1, \dots, p_{T} \in \Delta_K} & \quad \sum_{t=1}^{T} U_t(p_t) \\
\text{s.t.} & \quad \sum_{t = 1}^T \langle g_{t, k}, u - p_t \rangle \leq \epsilon, \quad \forall k\in[K].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Remark.} While $g_{t, k}$ in principle needs to be estimated using counterfactual allocations as well, this formulation allows us to assume that $U_{t, k}$ is approximately static around a given $t$ and to estimate $g_{t,k}$ by regressing on the values of $U_{t, k}$ of previous $H$ steps (see \Cref{sec:curv_est}).

\subsection{Primal-Dual Curvature Matching}
\vspace{-0.05in}
\Cref{eq:constrained_optimization_problem} is an online optimization problem with linear constraints, which allows us to employ Primal-Dual Mirror Descent (PDMD)~\citep{Beck2003MirrorDA,mahdavi2011trading,jenatton2015adaptivealgorithmsonlineconvex}. Generally, PDMD guarantees sublinear regret under convex constraints by controlling the allocation via an exponential update rule
\begin{equation} \label{eq:primal_update}
p_{t+1,j}
= 
\frac{p_{t,j}\exp(\eta_p s_{t,j})}
{\sum_{\ell=1}^Kp_{t,\ell}\exp(\eta_p s_{t,\ell})},
\end{equation}
where $s_{t, j}$ incorporates both the first-order information $G_t$ of the primal objective~\Cref{eq:bg_telescope} and a dual variable $\lambda_t$ that accumulates historical lag.
\begin{align}
& G_t = \left[ g_{t, 1}, \dots, g_{t, K} \right]^T, 
\qquad 
\text{where }
g_{t, k} = \nabla U_{t, k}(p_t) \quad \forall k \in [K]
\label{eq:dual_weighted_marginal_utility_vectorized} \\
& \lambda_{t+1, k} = \left[ \lambda_{t, k} + \eta_\lambda \left( \langle g_{t, k}, u - p_t \rangle - \epsilon / T \right) \right]_+,\qquad 
\text{where }  [x]_+ := \max\{x, 0\}
\label{eq:dual_update}
\end{align}
$G_t$ contains local information on how changes in the allocation $p_t$ affect task-wise policy improvement $U_{t, k}$ and $\lambda_{t}$ encodes how much Equation~\ref{eq:constraint_upper_bound} has been violated. The exponential logits $s_{t}$ are then combined in the following vectorized form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:s_combined}
s_t
=
G_t^\top \mathbf{1} + G_t^\top \lambda_t
\end{equation}
where the term $G_t^\top \mathbf{1}$ captures the first-order marginal utility of each allocation coordinate, while
$G_t^\top \lambda_t$ corrects for lag using a dual-weighted marginal utility.
% the second-order control as it encapsulates the gradient information $g_{t, k}$ twice. 
We summarize the algorithm in \Cref{alg:pdcm_jacobian}. 

\paragraph{Remark.} We note that as we chose \Cref{eq:constraint_upper_bound} to be the constraint, $G_t$ becomes the only quantity we need to estimate throughout the algorithm, a crucial design for the algorithm's tractability and simplicity. Because of this reliance on $G_t$, we name this algorithm \textbf{Primal-Dual Curvature Matching} (PDCM)\footnote{We recognize that it could be confusing to name the algorithm ``curvature matching'' when $G_t$ is just the first-order information of $U_{t, k}$. Meanwhile, curvature is conventionally used to refer to second-order information. We argue that $G_t$ is the second-order information of the total improvement $J(\pi_{T}) - J(\pi_0)$.}. The adaptive allocation in PDCM is intended to exploit temporal variation in task-wise marginal utility. The following result shows that this adaptivity remains asymptotically competitive with any fixed feasible allocation, including the uniform mixture, while simultaneously controlling cumulative task-wise lag.

\begin{theorem}[Constrained no-regret of PDCM]
\label{thm:pdcm_no_regret}
Suppose that each task-wise utility $U_{t,k}$ is twice-differentiable; and its Jacobian and the cumulative dual lag $\lambda_t$ are bounded. Let
\[
p_T^\star
\in
\arg\max_{p\in \Delta^K}
\sum_{t=1}^T U_t(p)
\]
denote the best fixed feasible allocation in hindsight. If
$\eta_p,\eta_\lambda=\Theta(T^{-1/2})$, then update rule \Cref{eq:primal_update} satisfies
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=1}^T
\left[
\sum_{k=1}^K U_{t,k}(p_T^\star) - \sum_{k=1}^K U_{t,k}(p_t)
\right]
\leq
O(\sqrt{T}),
\end{equation}
while, for every task $k$,
\begin{equation}
\left[
\sum_{t=1}^T
\left(
U_{t,k}(u)-U_{t,k}(p_t)-\epsilon / T
\right)
\right]_+
\leq
O(\sqrt{T}).
\end{equation}
Consequently, both the average regret and the average excess counterfactual lag vanish at rate $O(T^{-1/2})$. We provide a proof in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}.
\end{theorem}

What is left is the estimation of the policy improvement gradient $\hat{G}_t$. For that, we need a fast and tractable way of estimating the policy improvement $U_{t, k}$ and its gradient with respect to $p_t$.

\subsection{Policy Improvement Estimation}
\vspace{-0.05in}
The performance difference lemma~\citep{Kakade2002ApproximatelyOA} expresses the task-wise policy improvement $U_{t,k}$ as an expectation, over rollouts drawn from $\pi_t$, of the advantage $A_{\pi_t}$ reweighted by the importance ratio $\pi_{t+1}/\pi_t$. This makes $U_{t,k}$ a convenient target for off-policy evaluation~\citep{ope2000,gu2026actorcuratorcoadaptivecurriculumlearning}: we reuse the rollouts already generated for the training batch and estimate the task-wise policy improvement as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:policy_improvement_estimation}
\widehat U_{t,k}
=
\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}
\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_i\mid x_i)}
{\pi_{t}(y_i\mid x_i)}
A_{\pi_{t}}(y_i\mid x_i),
\end{equation}
where $(x_i, y_i)$ are the $i$-th state-action pair for task $k$ in the training batch on step $t$, $N_k$ is the number of task-$k$ samples in the current training batch. We estimate $A_{\pi_t}$ using the z-score estimator used by group-based RL algorithms such as GRPO~\citep{grpo2024} and GSPO~\citep{gspo2025}.

Compared to the standard RL post-training loop, this estimation only requires an additional forward pass of the improved policy $\pi_{t+1}$ to compute the importance sampling ratio $\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_i\mid x_i)}{\pi_{t}(y_i\mid x_i)}$. This is a negligible overhead compared to the cost of rollout generation or backpropagation.

\subsection{Curvature Estimation}
\vspace{-0.05in}

\label{sec:curv_est}
What is left is to estimate the allocation-response Jacobian $g_{t, k}$. Estimating this quantity also requires a counterfactual evaluation of the policy improvement around $p_t$. However, by maintaining a history of task-wise improvement vectors $\{\widehat U_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{t-1}$ and allocations $\{p_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{t-1}$, we can approximate the allocation-response Jacobian $g_{t, k}$ by local regression. We opt for the Theil-Sen estimator~\citep{Theil1992} for its robustness to noisy $\hat{U}_{t, k}$ when the allocation $p_{t, k}$ is low.
\vspace{-0.01in}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:theil-sen}
\widehat g_{t,kj}
=
\operatorname*{median}_{\substack{1\le a<b\le t-1\\ |p_{b,j}-p_{a,j}|>\delta}}
\frac{\widehat U_{b,k}-\widehat U_{a,k}}{p_{b,j}-p_{a,j}} .
\qquad
\forall k\in[K], j\in[K],
\end{equation}
where \(\delta>0\) is a minimum allocation-separation threshold used to avoid unstable slopes. Additionally, to prevent stale $p_t$, we apply dithering with decaying strength to
$p_t$ before sampling (see Line~\ref{alg:dirchlet}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:pdcm_jacobian}).
Here, $p_t$ denotes the controller's nominal allocation, while
$\tilde{p}_t$ denotes the dithered allocation actually used to sample
the training batch.
%Additionally, to prevent high variance estimation due to lack of exploration by the algorithm, we apply decaying Dirchelet noises to $p_{t}$ before sampling (see Line~\ref{alg:dirchlet}, \Cref{alg:pdcm_jacobian}).
